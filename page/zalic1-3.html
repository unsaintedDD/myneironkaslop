Parts of a ship and her measurement

1. What is the tonnage of a ship?

The measurement of her weight, size or capacity.

2. What types of tonnage do you know?

Gross Tonnage, Net Tonnage, and Deadweight Tonnage.

3. What is the difference between gross tonnage and net tonnage?

Gross tonnage is the moulded volume of all enclosed spaces. Net tonnage is the volume used to carry cargo, calculated by deducting non-cargo spaces from the gross tonnage.

4. What is deadweight tonnage?

The weight of all the contents a vessel is capable of carrying when loaded to the summer mark (cargo, stores, equipment, fresh/potable water, lubricating oil, and fuel).

5. What types of displacement do you know?

Light Displacement and Loaded Displacement.

6. What types of cargo spaces do you know?

Bale Space, Grain Space, and Oil Space.

7. What is meant by grain space?

The volume of the cargo holds that can be used to carry dry bulk cargo.

8. What is meant by bale space?

The volume of the cargo holds that can be used to carry general cargo.

9. What is meant by oil space?

98% of the total volume of the wet bulk tanks.

10. What is ullage?

The empty space (remaining 2%) on top of the liquid level that prevents a tank from overflowing when the oil expands due to heat.

11. What are moulded dimensions?

The distance between two points measured inside of a shell plating.

12. What is the inside width of a ship called?

Moulded breadth.

13. When is it especially important to take into consideration the beam of a ship?

It is an important factor to obtain a clearance to proceed.

14. Why do the port authorities have to know the ship’s LOA?

Before a berth along an embankment is allocated.

15. What is the construction waterline?

The line to which the ship may be loaded in summer.

16. What is meant by the following abbreviations: FP, AP and LBP?

FP (Fore-perpendicular) is the vertical line through the point where the Construction Waterline and stem intersect. AP (Aft-perpendicular) goes through the rudderstock. LBP (Length Between Perpendiculars) is measured between FP and AP to determine stability.

17. In which type of water is the draft largest? Why?

In fresh water, because fresh water is less dense than salt water.

18. Where could the highest point of the vessel be? Why is it important to know this?

The highest point is the mast (air draft). It is important to know as it is a factor to obtain clearance to proceed under bridges, power lines, and cranes.

19. When is it necessary to state your freeboard? Why?

When loading the vessel, because it indicates the safe loading limit and ensures that the vessel is not overloaded.

20. What is trim?

The difference between the draught at the stem (forward) and the draught at the stern.

21. When can we say that the vessel is trimmed by the stern/trimmed by the head?

Trimmed by the head when loaded with cargo and the draught forward is larger. Trimmed by the stern when loaded with cargo and the draught aft is larger.

22. What do we need load lines for?

They show the maximum load a ship can carry (the original "Plimsoll line").

23. What do draft marks show?

They measure the distance between the bottom of the hull (the keel) and the waterline.

All the dimensions of the ship and her stability (video)

1. What do you call the height of a hull from the highest point of its main deck to its lowest point?

Depth.

2. What is the difference between the centerline and baseline?

The centerline is a vertical plane that runs the length of the ship at the midpoint of its beam. The baseline is a horizontal plane perpendicular to the centerline, located at the lowest point of the hull.

3. What is keel?

The principal structural member of a ship running lengthwise along the centerline from bow to stern, to which the ship's frames are attached.

4. What is K? Where is it located?

K is the lowest point of the keel, serving as the point from which vertical distances are measured. It is located at the intersection of the centerline and the baseline.

5. What is the difference between the waterline and Design Water Line?

The waterline is the intersection of the water's surface with the hull. The Design Water Line (DWL) specifically represents the waterline of a ship under full load (maximum draft conditions) on an even keel.

6. What do you call the horizontal point halfway between the forward and aft perpendiculars?

Midships.

7. What directions are distances on ships measured in?

Longitudinally, transversely, and vertically.

8. How are longitudinal/ transverse/ vertical distances measured?

Longitudinally: From the forward perpendicular, aft perpendicular, or midships. Transversely: Port or starboard from the centerline (+/-). Vertically: Upward from the baseline or lowest point of the keel.

Navigational Aids

1. What are the main methods of navigation at sea?

The four basic methods of navigation at sea are Piloting, Dead Reckoning, Electronic Navigation, and Celestial Navigation.

2. What are buoys used for?

Buoys are used to indicate the side on which a ship should pass when moving in a given direction (by using simple arrangements of colors, shapes, numbers, and lights).

3. What are the measurements of water depths called?

The measurements of water depths are called soundings.

4. How is the ship navigated in piloting?

In piloting, the navigator directs a vessel by observing landmarks on the Earth's surface, taking visual bearings of conspicuous landmarks using the ship’s compass, measuring water depths (soundings), and taking observations of celestial bodies at regular intervals using the ship’s sextant.

5. What landmarks are used to navigate a ship?

Lighthouses, beacons, buoys, prominent rocks, cliffs, and church spires.

6. What are bearings?

A bearing is the horizontal angle between an object and a reference point, used to determine (fix) a ship's position.

7. How does the navigator determine a ship's position in dead reckoning?

By keeping a careful account (reckoning) of the distance and direction of travel from a known position called the point of departure.

8. How does the navigator find a ship's position in electronic and celestial navigation?

  • Electronic navigation: With the aid of electronic devices such as radar and others.
  • Celestial navigation: By observing the sun, moon, planets, and stars.

9. What is the difference between course and heading?

The course is the intended direction of the ship's travel, while the heading is the direction in which the ship is pointed at any given time.

10. What is a track?

The track (or course made good) is the direction of a straight line between a point of departure and a present position.

11. What is an instrument for measuring the speed of a ship called?

A ship's log (or maritime log).

12. Why are logbooks important?

They keep written accounts of voyages (the ship's course, speed, distance travelled, and weather information) and may serve as sources of data used in preparing navigational charts.

13. What is a knot?

A knot is a unit of speed that means velocity in nautical miles per hour.

14. What units of distance do you know?

  • Nautical mile: 6080 feet (1852 meters).
  • Cable: One-tenth of a nautical mile (used for short distances).
  • Fathom: 6 feet (used for measuring the depth of the sea).

15. What is tracking?

Tracking means monitoring, or following objects underway.

16. What is the chronometer/sextant used for?

  • Chronometer: To tell the exact time and help determine a ship's longitude.
  • Sextant: To calculate a ship's latitude by measuring the angle of the sun or of a star above the horizon.

17. Why is radar essential for navigation? What is it used for? What does it show?

  • Why it is essential: It identifies, tracks, and positions other vessels in order to navigate the ship from one point to another safely.
  • What it is used for: To calculate range and bearing of a target, determine speed and course, maintain a safe course, and fix the ship’s position.
  • What it shows: The screen displays all the targets within the radar range.

18. What is ARPA used for?

ARPA is used to calculate an object's course, speed, and distance, thereby knowing if there is a risk of collision.

19. Which system identifies and locates other vessels in the vicinity? What information does it provide?

The Automatic Identification System (AIS). It provides the vessel’s identification (IMO number, call sign), position, course, heading, speed, destination, draft, ETA, and DG.

20. What is a nautical chart used for?

Charts are used to plan voyages and navigate ships safely and economically. They give essential information on depths of water and heights of land, navigational hazards, aids to navigation, tides and currents, anchorage areas, VTS, and the nature of the seabed.

21. What is the modern alternative for a paper chart? What other navigational equipment is it interfaced with?

The modern alternative is ECDIS (Electronic Chart Display and Information System). It is interfaced with GPS, AIS, ARPA, echo sounder, etc.

22. What are the advantages of GPS?

It helps a navigator clearly understand what routes to take and what courses to avoid, gives accurate information, and is not much affected by weather conditions.

23. What is the difference between a magnetic compass and a gyrocompass?

A magnetic compass points to the magnetic North, while a gyrocompass is not affected by the magnetic field and is used to find the true North.

24. What is a helm used for?

A helm is used to switch to manual steering when needed.

Navigation Bridge (video)

1. Why is the bridge wing considered to be a critical vantage point?

It is a critical vantage point for berthing or docking because it allows the Captain and Harbor Pilot to gauge the position of the ship.

2. How many lifebuoys are there on each side of the bridge wing?

There are two lifebuoys on each side of the bridge wing.

3. What is a remote panel? Where is it located?

It is a panel containing controls and indicators, located on both the Port and Starboard Bridge wings.

4. What indicators and controls are there on a remote panel?

It has controls for the bow thrusters, and indicators for speed, engine rpm, rate of turn, heading, compass, mic, and speaker.

5. What is used for taking bearings of objects or celestial bodies?

A Gyro Repeater used in conjunction with an azimuth ring.

6. Where are the fire hydrants, fire extinguishers and fireboxes located?

Fireboxes and fire hydrants are all over the ship (one on each side of the bridge wing), and CO2 extinguishers are inside the bridge.

7. What can be found inside the navigation bridge?

A CO2 extinguisher, a pantry for snacks and drinks, a pilot chair, and the center console with electronic navigation equipment.

8. How does an EPIRB work?

It is installed in an open space so that if the ship sinks, it floats up and indicates the position via radio beacon.

9. What is a SART used for?

When activated, it allows other ships to locate the vessel on their radars.

10. What types of plans are onboard ships?

Fire plan, Damage Control Plan, and Dangerous Goods Plan.

11. Where is the most important type of navigation equipment located?

On the center console.

12. What does GMDSS consist of?

It consists of VHF 1, 2, and 3, MF/HF, Sat C, Sat F, and handheld UHF radios.

13. What is the difference between VHF, MF/HF and UHF?

VHF reaches up to 40 nautical miles, MF/HF can reach hundreds of miles depending on frequency and weather, and UHF is limited to line of sight.

14. What is the telegraph transmitter linked to?

It is linked directly down to the engine room's telegraph.

15. What program should be used for some extreme maneuvers of a vessel?

Program Bypass.

16. What is DGPS used for?

It is used for positioning.

17. When do we switch to manual hand steering?

When precision is needed.

18. What does the pilot door indicator show?

It shows if the pilot doors are completely closed.

19. What does the watertight door indicator show?

It shows the status of the watertight doors for the whole ship.

20. What types of communication are there within a ship?

Internal telephone, sound-powered internal telephone, and PA system.
